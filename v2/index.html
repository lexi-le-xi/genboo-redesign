When students build a robot

What happens when four high-school students take a humanoid from a box of parts to its first step, and learn every subject it takes along the way.

The beginning

For most students, math, physics, and coding arrive as separate classes, each with its own worksheet and its own test. Each one makes sense. Together, they rarely add up to anything a student actually cares about.

Yuanzhe Dong saw this up close. He builds AI at NVIDIA, and he had tried the usual tutoring for his own son. The lessons didn’t stick.

So he tried something different. Instead of teaching subjects, he started building a robot with the students, and suddenly the subjects had a reason to exist. Geometry was how a knee bends. Circuits were why a joint wouldn’t move. Code was how you make it stand up.

In 2025, a few parent friends joined in, and a garage project in Menlo Park became a program. This is what a team of four builds in eight weeks, and everything they learn on the way.

Weeks 1–3

A body

It starts as a box of 3D-printed parts and servo motors. Students build one limb first, because one limb teaches almost everything: joints, alignment, fasteners, torque. Then arms, legs, torso, and head.

LearnsMechanical engineering, reading assembly drawings, degrees of freedom.

Week 4

Nerves

A power system, an onboard computer, a servo bus, a motion sensor. Every wire is labeled and checked with a multimeter before anything turns on, because bad wiring beats good code every time.

LearnsCircuits, power, sensors, electrical safety.

Weeks 5–6

Reflexes

Power on, give every servo an ID, calibrate every joint. Then the first real program: move joint 3 by 10 degrees. It doesn’t land exactly on 10. Explaining why is the lesson.

LearnsLinux, Python, logging, the gap between a command and reality.

Week 7

A twin

The same robot, rebuilt in the MuJoCo physics simulator. Students run one motion in both worlds and compare. A simulation is a controlled approximation of reality, and they learn exactly where it’s wrong.

LearnsPhysics simulation, open- vs. closed-loop control, keyframe motion.

Week 8

Learning

Only now, AI. What is a policy? A reward? Students watch a policy train in simulation, change one reward, and see what the robot does differently. AI sits on top of everything they just built, not in place of it.

LearnsReinforcement learning: policies, rewards, observations, actions.

All eight weeks

Four people

None of it happens alone. Four students share one robot, split into sub-teams, rotate between operating and checking, and stand up together at the final showcase to explain what worked, and what didn’t.

LearnsCollaboration, engineering notebooks, presenting to an audience.

By week eight, you’ll have touched mechanical engineering, electronics, programming, control, simulation, and AI. Not as six classes. As one machine that needed all of them.

Why it matters

What a robot teaches that a class can’t

The robot is the vehicle. These three things are what we actually hope you walk away with.

#1 OWN

A project of their own

Most schoolwork, as Paul Graham puts it, is neither a project nor one’s own. Here, students take something from nothing to working and own every failure and fix on the way. Once you’ve gone from zero to one, you know you can do it again.

From a box of parts to first motion.

#2 WONDER

Curiosity that compounds

When AI can explain anything on demand, knowing things is cheap. Wanting to know is rare. Nobody has to assign the reading on torque when your robot’s knee keeps buckling. That pull is the engine of everything you’ll go on to teach yourself.

Mechanics → electronics → control → AI.

#3 TOGETHER

Four people, one robot

No one builds a humanoid alone. Teams split into sub-teams, rotate roles, and debug together. Each keeps their own engineering notebook, so the robot is shared but the understanding is personal.

One mentor for every team of four.
